# The Brackenwood Cutter’s Handbook (Excerpt)

**Issued By:** The Oak Hall, Tarrow-on-the-Reed
**Material:** Thick, wax-coated canvas pages designed to survive rain and sap. Found in the pockets of most licensed logging crews.

**Section IV: On the Felling of Quercus ferrum (Iron-Oak)**

It cannot be stressed enough to new crews that cutting Iron-Oak is not akin to harvesting common pine. Iron-Oak achieves its density by growing in places where the land has been heavily anchored by human memory. When you cut an old-growth Iron-Oak, you are not just felling timber; you are severing a taproot of held memory.

**The Memory Wake:**
When the trunk finally snaps, the sudden release of the tree’s grip on the earth will trigger a localized, violent Fast Drift (a "Stir" or "Wake").

*   **Preparation:** Before the felling cut is completed, all oxen must be unyoked and moved at least a bowshot away. Tools must be placed in carts, not left on the ground, lest the soil swallow them during the Stir.
*   **The Strike:** The Oak-Mark (the Wheel and Wave seal) MUST be struck into the stump *before* the tree falls. This seal acts as a bureaucratic anchor, a minor naming that tells the land the stump still has a purpose, mitigating the violence of the Wake.
*   **The Fall:** When the tree begins to tip, the crew must run up-slope. Do not watch the tree hit the ground. The resulting geographical shudder will cause intense vertigo. Wait until the smell of bruised dirt and ozone dissipates before returning to limb the trunk.

**Section V: On the Oak-Mark, Windfall, and the Three Branches**

Let no crew leave Tarrow without understanding what hangs on the wax. The Oak-Mark is a lead-and-wax seal struck in the Hall’s own pattern: a wheel and a wave. The wheel is the carting; the wave is the water that carries it. Struck into the standing trunk before the fall, it writes the Hall’s name into the land, and the land, being a punctilious clerk, honors it. Cut a standing oak unmarked and you have not merely broken your charter — you have been caught *stealing shade*. An iron-oak takes two lifetimes to mature, and every year of those two lifetimes the tree was holding the ground still for the rest of us.

Windfall — wood downed by storm, wind, or the land’s own turning — is free to take. It is never free of custom. When you dress a windfall, leave **three leafy branches at the stump**, so the next cutter to pass knows the fall was the wood’s own doing and not a thief’s apology. Three, and no trimming the count: one for the cart, one for the crew, one for the wood. The Hall can smell a stripped stump from a market’s distance, and the Hall price of a stripped stump is not paid in coin.

*From "The Builders and the Breakers: Tradecraft of the Reaches"*
