# Excerpt from "Remedies of the Oaker Hills"

**Author:** Mother Cotta, Midwife and Herbalist of Nine Acre
**Date:** Copied and updated for three generations; this edition approx. twenty years old.
**Material:** A greasy, well-thumbed leather book, kept near the hearth in most Tops households.

**On the Treatment of Drift-Sickness (The "Vertigos")**
When a traveler stares too long at unanchored land during a Fast Drift, the eyes and the stomach disagree on the shape of the world. The patient will become pale, unable to stand, and will often vomit.

*Remedy:* Do not put the patient to bed in a dark room; the dark will only unmoor them further. Seat them in a sturdy chair (iron-oak is best, as it feels heavy to the touch). Place a single hearth-grade lantern exactly three paces in front of them. Command them to stare at the flame and nothing else. Brew a tea of willow-bark and ginger. Do not let them sleep until they can confidently point to magnetic North.

**On the Ingestion of Blue Moss (Cyanobryum)**
It is a common mistake among new foragers to brew Blue Moss thinking it is a winter-mint. It is not poisonous, but it induces a temporary, profound localized amnesia. The patient will physically forget their own name, their trade, and their history for roughly one hour.

*Remedy:* Panic is the true danger here. If the patient runs into the Wilds while unmoored from their own identity, the ground will quickly forget them too. Have the patient grip a tool they use every day (a carving knife, a hammer, a spindle). The muscle memory will anchor their mind. Sit with them and recite their lineage aloud until they can finish the sentence for you. 

**On the Wearing-Away of Small Memories**
The land is not the only thing that forgets. Folk who live alone at the wood's edge — woodsmen, widow-keepers, lamplighters on long routes — will lose a small thing, a piece at a time: the taste of their own bread, the tune their mother hummed, the name of a long-dead dog. Nothing so grand as what the Blue Moss takes. It is the drift wearing at them the way wind wears at a fence post, and the older the loss, the harder the mending.

*Remedy:* Render tallow and stew it with comfrey root into a stiff salve. Rub it well into the hands and the wrists at night — the hands keep more memory than the head does, in my experience — and wrap them in wool. Come morning, set the patient to work they have done a thousand times: kneading dough, carving, winding a spindle. Talk to them of small matters while the hands are busy, and make them answer. Do not ask after the lost thing itself; asking drives it further off. It comes home the way a stray cat does, when it is not summoned.

*From "Blood, Sap, and Memory: Medical and Survival Texts of the Reaches"*
