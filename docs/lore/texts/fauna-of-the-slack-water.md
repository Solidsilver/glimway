# "Fauna of the Slack Water: An Observer's Guide"

**Author:** Master Kaelen, Naturalist of the Merrow Saltings
**Date:** Published 15 years ago.
**Location Found:** Elara Quill's personal satchel, heavily dog-eared and water-stained.

**Section: Adaptations to Geographic Fluidity**

**The Tide-Fox (Vulpes fluens)**
Unlike the common red fox of the stable western hills, the Tide-Fox of the deep Wilds has evolved entirely alongside the Drift. It is leaner, entirely deaf to low-frequency sounds, but possesses an extraordinary spatial memory that functions independently of physical landmarks. 

Most fascinating is the Tide-Fox's caching behavior. A common fox buries a kill by a distinctive rock to find it later. A Tide-Fox intentionally buries its surplus in zones of *fast drift* (often just before the turning of the Autumn Wick). It does not return to the physical coordinates of the cache. Instead, it waits for the "White Quiet" of winter, when the deep drift invariably surfaces what it has swallowed. The fox allows the land to refrigerate the meat, returning to the general region weeks later to collect the bounty when the ground gives it back. It uses the very instability of the earth as a larder.

**The False-Path Weaver (Araneae erroris)**
A ground-dwelling arachnid that spins horizontal, silver-grey webs across the moss. It weaves these webs exclusively in the exact patterns of human direction-marks or cart-ruts. We must theorize that the Weaver understands that humans are drawn to familiar shapes. The web does not catch humans, of course; it catches the carrion-beetles and moths that feed on the refuse travelers leave behind when they follow a familiar mark into a dead end.

**The Whitequiet Jackdaw (Monedula remissa)**
A grey-hooded corvid of the deep Wilds, seldom seen except at the turning of the season. When the outer country gives back what the deep drift has swallowed — and it gives back most generously at the White Quiet — the jackdaw flies the unsettled ground in slow, patient lines and exacts its toll of trinkets: a dropped glove, a bright road-nail, a candle saved too long for its lighting. It carries these up to the nest and sets them out in the open air, plainly arranged, and defends the display against all comers save one. We have watched a jackdaw stand aside, perfectly calm, while a weeping shepherd took back her own thimble. We must theorize that the bird knows the difference between a treasure and a memory, and that it has no use for the latter once it is claimed.

*Pencilled on the flyleaf, in a hand that is not Master Kaelen's:*
`The Saltings chart this on drift-tables — what the land gives back, and when. Kaelen has the bird half right: it does not read the cold, it reads the letting-go. Ride the giving-back. Never chase it. —E.Q.`
