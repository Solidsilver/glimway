# Log of the Bark "Wind-Hewn" (The Fog-Coast Expedition)

**Author:** Captain Vane, commissioned by the Oak Hall
**Date:** Forty-two years ago.
**Location Found:** The Maritime Archives of Tarrow-on-the-Reed.

*Background: Seeking new sources of timber, the Oak Hall funded an expedition down the Reed River, past the Merrow Saltings, and into the great eastern sea, looking for the legendary "Uncut Isles."*

**Day 14 at Sea:**
Land sighted. A massive, towering coastline of black rock and dense, virgin forest. The timber here is untouched. The Oak-Master aboard wept at the size of the canopies. We dropped anchor in a deep, sheltered crescent bay at dusk. The crew is in high spirits. We make landfall at dawn.

**Day 15 (Morning):**
The sea is a constant. It shifts, it rolls, but it is honest water. Land is supposed to be the anchor. But the land here flows like the sea.

I came up on deck at first light. The crescent bay is gone.
While we slept, the two peninsulas that formed the mouth of the bay simply drifted together and locked shut like teeth. We are no longer anchored in a bay; we are trapped in a landlocked saltwater lake.

The Oak-Master went ashore in a rowboat, claiming the timber was too valuable to ignore. He took a hatchet and struck a tree. The moment the iron bit into the bark, the entire shoreline shuddered. A fast-drift, violent and immediate, triggered by the sudden, sharp memory of the cut.

The beach folded inward. The trees swallowed the rowboat. The Oak-Master vanished into the treeline, screaming, as the forest physically carried him away at the speed of a galloping horse.

**Day 18:**
We have been stranded in this lake for three days. We burn our lamps bright on the deck all night, trying to hold the water open, but our ship's light is too small for the sheer mass of this primordial, unremembered continent. This land has never known a human foot, never felt a lantern's light, and it resents our shape.

The navigator believes the moon will force a Turning tomorrow. If the peninsulas open, I will order the anchor cut and we will ride the tide out. We will not return. Leave the wood to the Wilds.

**Day 19:**
The navigator was right about the moon. At slack water the two peninsulas ground apart like millstones remembering their duty, and the bay stood open for the better part of an hour. I ordered the anchor cut at the ebb and we rode out stern-first, lamps lit, no man speaking.

I understand our failure now, and it is not the land's fault. A lamp holds nothing it is not told to hold. Our light burned hollow the whole while we lay in that water — warm, bright, and pinning nothing — because we had no words for that coast. No road ever named out there, no shrine, no keeper's tongue. The land is not cruel. We were a word it did not know.

The bay healed shut behind us like water over a stone. I have entered the Oak-Master as lost ashore. Let the isles keep their timber.

*(Appended to the log in the Archives, in a clerk's fair hand, under the Hall's wheel-and-wave seal:)*

INQUIRY INTO THE VOYAGE OF THE BARK WIND-HEWN. CLOSED.
The Hall finds the loss of the Oak-Master an act of the Wilds and no fault of the captain's. The crew are paid to the day and bound to quiet. No further charters will issue for waters past the Merrow Saltings; the Hall will not purchase timber at the price of remembering what it cuts. Iron-oak is to be had nearer home by patience.
That is the Hall price of wood.
— The Magistrates of the Oak Hall

*From "The Liar's Art: Cartography and Exploration in the Reaches"*
