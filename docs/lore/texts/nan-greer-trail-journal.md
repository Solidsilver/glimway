# A Page from Nan Greer’s Trail Journal

**Author:** Nan Greer, Lamplighter
**Date:** Thirty-one years ago (Written on the "Lull Run", between the Two Storms; some of it set down after, from memory).
**Material:** A pocketbook stained with dirt, blood, and charcoal.

**The fourth day of the lull, or the fifth. I lost count in the white:**
Tam caught his hand in the yoke-chain when the left ox bolted at a shifting shadow. Two fingers crushed. The knuckle on the index finger is powder. 

He didn’t scream, just went white as milk and sat down in the mud. Hollis had to pry the chain links apart with a pry-bar to get the hand free. 

We are three days from the nearest Chirurgeon and the air smells like the second storm is already breathing down our necks. I did what Wenna taught me. I took a drop of storm-grade amber, held it in a spoon over the campfire until it melted, and poured it directly over the crushed knuckles. 

Tam screamed then. Bett had to sit on his legs. But the amber hardened instantly, forming a perfect, rigid cast around the broken bones. It stopped the bleeding, and the faint light of the amber is keeping the flesh anchored against the swelling. He can’t move it, but he won't lose the fingers. 

Told him he'll have to drive the ox with his left hand. He told me to go to hell. The boy is fine. 

**The day it came (the sixth, by Hollis's count; I had stopped counting):**
It came three days early, and it did not come as weather. Hollis calls it a stir — wood moving like weather. The hills were walking this morning, slow as oxen, and we were still short of the crossing.

I read route marks the way other folk read faces, and the marks had gone strange. The weave above the water was slack, like a face gone numb down one side. I called to hurry the teams. We were not fast enough.

At the Tangle, the ground beneath Orrin's bridge shifted and tore, like a page ripped out of a book. The bridge went down with the carts. Bett and Dorrit went with them.

I have written it, and it stays written. That is all a journal is for, in the end. Dorrit, who set our signpost crooked her first day in the village. Bett, who sang flat all week on purpose, just to make her brother groan.

Hollis stood at the edge with his whole back shaking. Then he turned and said: four of us, and the road east, and the oil. Tam's cast held — a small gold coal in all that ruin. Joss blew his dented note so we would not lose one another in the flying dark.

Behind us, the chain went out lantern by lantern, the way a house goes dark when its people leave. I am a lamplighter. I have never in my life watched a chain die and kept my feet moving. We push for Sallow Ford.

*From "Blood, Sap, and Memory: Medical and Survival Texts of the Reaches"*
