# "Assessing the Barge-Knee Yields of the Eastern Reaches"

**Author:** Inspector Corlis, Tarrow Oak Hall
**Date:** Written 60 years ago.
**Location Found:** A bureaucratic filing cabinet in Tarrow-on-the-Reed. 

**Report to the Magistrates regarding Quercus ferrum (Iron-Oak):**

We have completed our survey of the Brackenwood sector. The density of the Iron-Oak harvested this season remains exceptional, though the biological mechanism behind its growth continues to baffle our arborists. 

Unlike standard oak, which requires nutrient-rich soil and ample sunlight to produce tight growth rings, *Quercus ferrum* exhibits its tightest, most impenetrable ring clusters in the most barren, heavily trafficked regions of the Wilds. 

Our surveyors have noted a curious correlation: the oldest, hardest trees—those whose timber sinks in water and fetches the highest Hall Price—are invariably found growing directly over the sites of abandoned campfires, ruined way-shrines, or along old footpaths. 

The prevailing theory among the Hall's botanists is that the ash from human fires heavily alkalizes the soil, stunting the tree's upward growth and forcing the cellular structure inward. We recommend dispatching crews to deliberately burn scrub-brush around saplings to replicate this effect.

*A note pinned to the report by a senior clerk:*
`Inspector Corlis's burn-crews achieved nothing but a forest fire. The local foragers claim the tree grows dense because it is "listening" to the campfires, and that the ground holds still for the tree only when a human has made a memory there. Keep this nonsense out of the official ledgers, but cease the burnings.`

*Folded with the report, in Inspector Corlis's own hand — a private memorandum, four years subsequent, never filed:*
`The alkaline theory is dead, and I will not spend another season proving it. What survives is the foragers' account, which I have tested at last. I have sawn or sampled thirty-six tight-ringed stumps across the Brackenwood, and every one stands within a stone's throw of a place where folk kept a light and kept returning to it: a way-shrine, a shepherd's hearth, a night-camp on the old hill road. Not ash. Attendance. The ground under such places appears to have held still, and the tree, given stillness, closes its rings like a fist. The foragers call this listening, which this office may not record. Recorded instead: recommend the Hall quietly purchase cutting rights along the old footpaths, and let the rest go unexplained.`

*From "Scholarly Texts of the Reaches: Science, Taxonomy, and Misunderstanding"*
