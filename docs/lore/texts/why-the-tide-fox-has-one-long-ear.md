# Why the Tide-Fox Has One Long Ear

*An origin myth favored by the Carter families, explaining both the nature of the Drift and the peculiar asymmetry of the local foxes.*

Long before the Carters brought their heavy wheels, the animals of the woods had to learn to live with the shifting earth. The Bear tried to hold the ground still by being heavy, but the ground simply rolled him over in his sleep. The Owl tried to hold it still by watching it all night, but she blinked at dawn, and her tree moved a mile.

But the Fox was clever. The Fox knew you cannot fight the Drift; you can only trick it.

One autumn, the Fox caught three fat rabbits. He ate one, but he wanted to save the other two for the White Quiet of winter. He knew if he buried them by a rock, the rock would wander off. 

So, the Fox found the fastest, angriest patch of Drift in the woods. He dropped the rabbits into the churning earth and let the ground swallow them. 

Then, the Fox sat down to wait for winter. But the Fox made a mistake. He watched the woods in front of him, trying to guess where the rabbits would pop back up. He stared so hard forward that he didn't hear the woods drifting *behind* him. A sallow-tree stepped backward and caught the Fox's left ear in its bark.

The Fox yelped and pulled, and pulled and yelped, stretching his left ear long and thin until he popped free. 

When winter finally came, the White Quiet settled in. The ground froze and gave back what it had swallowed, pushing the two fat rabbits right up through the frost, directly between the Fox's paws. 

The Fox learned his lesson. To this day, a Tide-Fox never looks straight ahead to see where it is going. It keeps one short ear pointed forward, but it swivels its long, stretched ear backward, listening to the ground it just left—because in the Wilds, what is behind you is always trying to catch up.

*(Note: This is why Silas carves his door-foxes with a long left ear. It is the ear that listens behind.)*

*(Copied into the margin of Pip's own story-book, in smaller and much neater handwriting: "A pretty tale, but the fox flatters you. The ground does not chase, and it is not angry; it simply has not been told about you. Learn the difference in the soil itself: if it churns like boiling porridge and smells of bruised roots, that is Fast Drift, and no amount of listening will save your boots. In the Saltings we do not beg the ground to remember—we ride it, with drift-tables. Still, the long ear is correct. Listen behind you. — E.Q.")*
