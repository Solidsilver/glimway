# The Sallow Ford Chirurgeon’s Field-Book

**Author:** Master Surgeon Fane, contracted by the Carters' Compact
**Date:** Written during the peak of the Great Carting, roughly fifty years ago.
**Location Found:** The Sallow Ford Guildhall.

**Entry: Iron-Oak Splinters and Trauma**
The woodcutters of the Brackenwood suffer injuries unlike any standard timber-camp. *Quercus ferrum* (Iron-Oak) is incredibly dense. When a wedge snaps or a saw binds and shatters, the resulting wood shrapnel behaves more like iron slag than timber. 

The human body cannot naturally suppurate or push out an Iron-Oak splinter. The wood is too stubborn. Left alone, the flesh will simply attempt to heal *around* it, often necrotizing. 
*Treatment:* The surgeon must cut deep and extract the splinter entirely with forceps. I have pulled splinters from carters' shoulders that were driven so hard they notched the collarbone. Clean the wound with boiled vinegar, for the sap of the tree will try to harden in the blood.

**Entry: Anchor-Burns (The Keeper's Blister)**
I treated Keeper Renn yesterday. During the last storm, the drift pulled so violently against the Amberwash route that the glass of his lantern heated to the point of glowing. In his desperation to hold the naming, he did not let go of the iron handle. 

The resulting burn is unique. The flesh is not merely scorched; it is stained a deep, translucent gold from the vaporized amber-oil. The pain is reportedly agonizing, as the burn site feels constantly "heavy." I applied a poultice of rendered tallow and crushed comfrey. It will scar, and the scar will likely glow very faintly in complete darkness. Keepers wear these scars with absurd pride.

**Entry: The Sinking Green**
The carter's saying runs: *green wood sinks, dry wood sings.* Iron-Oak cut green is heavier than the water it is laid upon — a fact the barge men of this Ford treat as a rumor until it drowns one of them. Not a month past, a hand named Cottle was pinning a raft of green knees in the shallows when the load rolled. A single crook took him to the bottom, and it wanted two men and a hook-rope to bring up man and timber together.

The leg was not broken, but the flesh was pulped from ankle to knee, and bruised the color of a storm sky. I have set a hundred crushings from chains and yokes; none of them compare to the weight of this. Green Iron-Oak does not bounce, does not shift, and does not care. I told the Compact's factors that no man should pin green knees in the water. They told me the tolls must flow. I have sewn shut enough of their tolls.

*A note set down secondhand, and given its due:* Carter folk trading at the toll speak of a western method for crushed bones — melted amber, poured over the ruin while it is still soft, which sets about the knots like a splint and holds the flesh against the swelling. I laughed the first time I heard it. Then a driver off the Tops showed me a wrist set so, the cast glowing faint as a live coal in the dark of my surgery, and the hand beneath it whole. The storm-grade amber such a cast requires would beggar my practice twice over, so I will not be trying it. I record it only because I have learned the west does not boast.

*From "Blood, Sap, and Memory: Medical and Survival Texts of the Reaches"*
