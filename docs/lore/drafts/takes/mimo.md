# mimo — the wider world and its history

Focus: what the lantern road connected Hearthwick to; the towns, river, hills
and peoples beyond the map; trade (iron-oak, amber); the carters; customs;
festivals; the calendar; sayings and songs; and a timeline from before
Hearthwick to now.

Thesis: the interesting thing about Hearthwick is not the Wood That Moves —
it is that Hearthwick is a **company town that lost its road**. It was built to
cut iron-oak and dig amber for a river market nine days away. Everything odd
about it (the grandmothers' ledgers, the empty hame on the gate, the way
everyone can quote the price of hinges) is bookkeeping grief. The wider world
is not decoration around the map. It is the reason the map exists.

---

## 1. The map beyond the map

```
                the Oaker Hills (charcoal, smallholdings)
                 Holt End · Nine Acre · the Stone-Walk cairns
                        |  the hill road (open Carting and Amberfall)
                        |
   Tarrow-on-the-Reed ==Sennick's Ferry== ...meadow... == Reed Mill (Finn)
   [Count House, Oak Hall]        (the old barge landing, now grass)
        |                                  |
        |  THE LANTERN ROAD (the Reed Road, closed 31 years) |
        |  Count Post → Amberwash → Mossy Arch → Frozen Pond |
        |  → Ashwatch Waystation (keeper's house, shrine lantern)
        |  → Brackenwood Path → Hearthwick east gate
        v
   the Reed bends south through the Wilds' edge (wandering worse every year)
```

**Tarrow-on-the-Reed.** Nine days by the hill road over the Tops; three by
water when the Reed still ran where the maps say. River market town: wharves,
the **Count House** (cargo counted, tolls punched, tallies kept), and the
**Oak Hall** (the iron-oak trade board that charters cutting and sets the
Hall price). Tarrow is where Hearthwick's oak and amber were sold and where
salt, iron, cloth, medicine and news came back. No one in the current village
has been there in thirty years and everyone has an opinion about it.

**Sennick's Ferry.** The Reed crossing on the road between Tarrow and the
Wilds. The Sennicks have run the chain-ferry for generations. They don't hold
with lanterns: the river doesn't hold its shape either and you don't see them
lighting it.

**The Oaker Hills / the Tops.** Charcoal burns and smallholdings over the
ridge: **Holt End**, **Nine Acre**. The hill road is long, steep, and honest —
it does not move. This is Hearthwick's only remaining outside contact. The
Tops carters who come over in Carting and Amberfall are who brought the player
to the square ("the traveler the carters mentioned"), and who carry Pip's
messages, Mara's ledgers and Orrin's hinge complaints onward.

**The Amberwash.** Not a place so much as a habit: the pale gravel scars in
old streambeds where amber shows after hard water, all through the Wilds and
down to the Reed. The amber crews walk these "by stone, not by path" —
cairn-marked, because paths lie out there.

**The Lantern Road proper** (what the player walks the first stretch of):
east gate → Brackenwood Path → Count Post → Amberwash → Mossy Arch → Frozen
Pond → Ashwatch Waystation → the hilltop shrine lamp. It ran on over the
shoulder to the Tops and down to the Ferry. Chain of lanterns, never allowed
to go dark in a row. That was the whole trick.

## 2. What the road carried

### Iron-oak

Slow oak from Brackenwood and the Wilds' edge. So dense green timber sinks.
It doesn't split for bridges, doesn't strip for wheel hubs, doesn't burn
quick in a brake block — the Tops folk use it for charcoal rings and the
Tarrow barge-builders pay silly money for "knees" (the natural crooks).

Cutting is chartered by the **Oak Hall**: an **oak-mark** (a lead-and-wax
seal in the Hall's pattern — a wheel and a wave) must be stamped into the
stump before the second cut. Cutting standing oak without a mark is
**"stealing shade"** — the phrase does the work of a law: you are stealing
shade from somebody's grandchildren, because a planted iron-oak outlives the
planter. Windfall may be taken free, but custom says leave **three leafy
branches at the stump** ("a hand of leaves") so the woodcutter coming after
you knows the fall was natural.

### Amber

Hardened tree-sap with a deep golden glow — the same stuff that makes the
lantern glass "amber glass." Two sources: dug from the Amberwash gravel after
floods, and **bled** in the tide weeks (Sap-wick and Amber-wick) from scored
iron-oak, which weeps a bright resin that hardens overnight.

Amber is sold by the **drop** (a small stone weight, about an ounce) and
graded by what it can hold alight:

- **candle-grade** — keeps a flame's light a few minutes after the flame is
  out (children's night-lights, cheap window lamps);
- **hearth-grade** — an hour; the village window-lamps at Amberwake;
- **storm-grade** — overnight; used for lantern glass and for the warden's
  sealed core. Rare. The autumn train of thirty-one years ago was carrying
  most of what the district had left.

Amber is why the road was worth building and why the closure hurt. Amber is
also the one thing the Wilds give back generously — Elara's notebooks and the
crews' cairns agree: the drift *loosens* gravel. It's the only kindness out
there and it is entirely accidental.

### What came back up the road

Salt. Iron — nails, tyres, hinges, which is why Orrin has opinions about the
Hall price of hinges. Cloth. Medicine. Glass beads (frosted green river
glass, sold by the string). **News.** And the Count House's punched tallies,
which mattered more than anyone admitted at the time.

### The carters and their Compact

The **Carters' Compact** is a road fraternity with halls at Tarrow and
Hearthwick. Hearthwick's hall is now Silas's timber shed over by the Commons;
the sign is still up (the Wheel and Wick, a wheel carved over a candle).

The Compact's three duties, sworn at entry and recited at Carting Day:

1. **Light** — never leave the chain dark. The last cart out lights behind
   itself and checks the wick ahead.
2. **Log** — log your load at the Count House both ends. No unlogged amber,
   no unmarked oak.
3. **Leave** — leave the road as you'd wish to find it. Cairns topped, ruts
   drained, gates shut.

Tolls are a **wheel-tax**: a copper per wheel per lantern-lit league, paid at
the Count Post or the Count House, receipted with a punched token. The Count
Post's iron stub and its token-hole are still out there in the moss.

Supersition, not rule, but every carter keeps it: **don't whistle up the
road.** Whistling after the third lantern "calls the road to move." Nobody
thinks the road is evil. They think it is busy.

### Customs that outlived the trade

- **The hame on the gate.** At Carting Day a polished horse-collar is hung on
  the Commons gate for the cart that never came home. Nobody says whose. It
  is polished every year by whoever finds it first. Lately that is Silas.
- **The last lantern.** However late the hour, one lantern in the square
  stays lit "for the last cart." It used to be literal.
- **The open account.** A trade account is not closed until its tally is
  settled at the Count House. Not by death, not by weather. (See Twist 1.)

## 3. The calendar: wicks, marks and turnings

Tarrow folk say "month." Up here a month is a **wick** — the stretch between
one trimming and the next — because the keeper used to trim the whole chain
on a schedule and the schedule was how people counted time.

**The twelve wicks**, from the thaw round:

`Thaw · Mud · Bud · Bloom · Light · Cart · Haze · Sap · Amber · Leaf · Smoke · Quiet`

**The four marks** (seasons), named for road work rather than weather:

| Mark | Wicks | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| **Mudrise** | Thaw, Mud, Bud | Breakup and freshet. Roads soft. The Breaking. |
| **Carting** | Bloom, Light, Cart | Long light. The Great Carting. The hill road opens. |
| **Amberfall** | Haze, Sap, Amber, Leaf | The sap tide, the crews, the amber harvest. |
| **Quiet** | Smoke, Quiet, Thaw* | Salting, charcoal, deep dark. Closure Night. |

\* locals overlap the year's seam; "the Quiet runs until the ice says
otherwise."

**The Turning.** The outer Wilds do not drift on a whim. They turn on a
schedule the village has learned to read — never quite the moon, never quite
the wick. **The notice board posts the date a day ahead:** "Dark of Amber-wick
— the outer Wilds will turn." Cairn-walkers clear their stones. Charts reset.
Elara packs. This is not a supernatural omen; it is closer to a ferry timetable
for a river that runs through land instead of water. (See Twist 2.)

### Festivals

**The Breaking** (Mudrise, at the ice-out). Everyone goes down to the Reed to
watch the ice go. Children float **candle hulls** — walnut-shell boats with
leaf sails and a stub of tallow. The hull that travels farthest is "the last
cart of winter." Finn hosts it under protest and has rules about downstream of
the wheel.

**Carting Day** (Carting, Cart-wick the sixth). Once the Tarrow fair where the
year's oak and amber were sold and the Hall price set. Now a Commons market
without customers: barter, cider, the empty hame on the gate, and Silas
pretending he doesn't cry during the reciting of the three duties. Tops carters
sometimes time their crossing to be here. It is the most cheerful sad day of
the year.

**Amberwake** (Amberfall, first night of Amber-wick). Every house sets a
window lamp with fresh-cut amber glass. The town glows. Inside, at the Long
Table, the **Empty Chair** — a place set, plate and glass, for the five who
went into the Tangle. Custom predates the Two Storms (it was originally for
the drowned and the moved-on), which the village finds comforting rather than
tasteless.

**Closure Night** (Quiet, the anniversary of the closing). Every lantern in
the village is lit and trimmed in one night; **the road beyond the last post
is deliberately left dark** — the one night the chain is broken on purpose.
Then the **Telling** at the Long Table: everyone recounts the wick's news, and
the keeper reads the coming year's turnings off the board. Mara calls it the
best night of the year and means it twice.

## 4. Sayings, songs, speech

### Sayings

- **"Two weaves and a break."** The closure mark. In speech: *a decision made
  and kept.* "You said you'd stop. Two weaves and a break, now."
- **"That's the Hall price."** Non-negotiable. Also used of stubborn people.
- **"Green wood sinks, dry wood sings."** Patience. Hurried work drowns.
- **"Leave the last lantern."** Don't close the door on anyone still out.
- **"Don't be the last cart."** Mild warning, not grim: don't push your luck.
- **"Mind the axle."** Don't overload the plan.
- **"As the Reed goes."** Things change; accept it. Finn says it like a
  curse word.
- **"Stealing shade."** Taking something that belongs to someone not born yet.
- **"One wick at a time."** The village answer to every big problem.
- **"It's a long way to Tarrow."** It's complicated / that's above my pay.
- **"Walk by stone, not by path."** Crew wisdom: paths move, cairns you just
  built are honest. Elara has it over her tent flap.

### Songs

**The Carters' Counting Song.** Work song, call-and-response, one verse per
lantern on the chain — which makes it a map. Pip knows it and sings it wrong
on purpose.

> One for the gate and two for the hill,
> three for the arch that leans still,
> four for the pond that never thaws,
> five for the waystation walls.
> *(and so on — the last verse is always improvised and always rhymes with
> "home")*

Game hook: as the player relights lanterns, the verses they hear from Pip (and
later from Silas, quietly, alone) grow.

**Leave the Last Lantern.** Lullaby, very old, sung at the Breaking and at
Closure Night without anyone deciding to.

> Leave the last lantern, the last lantern lit,
> leave the door on the latch and the fire low.
> The road is a promise and the road is long,
> and the last cart home still has to go.

After the Two Storms the last line is sometimes sung "the last cart home may
yet still go," and nobody admits they change it.

**Reed and Roll.** Finn's family mill song, originally a towpath chant:

> Reed will wander where the Reed will go,
> mind the wheel and mind the toe.
> Stone that's steady, water that's free —
> miller stands between, like me.

**Stone-still.** Skipping rhyme, Ashwatch:

> Stone-still, stone-still, who goes over Ashwatch hill?
> Bring a rubbing or bring a light —
> stone-still lets you through tonight.

## 5. The timeline

Years are given before "now" in the Count House's count (Tarrow's charter
year zero). Villagers mostly count by winters and events, which is why oral
accounts drift.

**Long before any of this.** The Wilds older than the village; iron-oak older
than the Wilds. The hilltop offering stone (the Old Shrine POI) with its
acorn offering — a pre-road custom of the amber crews: *leave something that
grows.* The Stone-Walk cairns on the Tops. The Reed running its old course
past where the barge landing would be.

**~155 years ago — the Settling.** Hearthwick founded on the hillside for the
iron-oak and the amber. Reed Mill built at the ford (Finn's family, seven
generations). The village keeps a hearth-lantern in the square from the first
winter; that is the tradition the player is restoring.

**~122 years ago — the Oak Charter.** Tarrow's Oak Hall chartered. Oak-marks,
the Count House, the wheel-tax. Iron-oak stops being a lumber camp and becomes
a trade with paperwork.

**~110 years ago — the Lantern Road.** The drift makes the old track
unreliable; the Compact and the Hall fund the chain of lamps from the Reed up
to the shrine. Ashwatch Waystation built — part relay stable, part keeper's
house, part customs shed. **The anchoring is folk knowledge, not wizardry:**
the crews noticed that a lit lamp holds the land around it to its shape the
way a stone holds a tent. So they lit a chain of lamps and got a road that
stayed. Rule of the chain: never two dark in a row.

**~85–40 years ago — the Great Carting.** Peak trade. The Commons becomes the
staging ground (the ruts are from this era). Carting Day grows from the fair.
Amberwake begins when someone in Tarrow starts shipping amber glass for the
road lamps — "the chain of gold." Silas grows up driving these roads.

**~36 years ago — the first unease.** The Reed begins to wander: old Fen,
Finn's father, notes the wheel slipping three feet in a season. The outer
Wilds turn more often than the wicks. Some lamps drift out of true. **Keeper
Hessa** (Mara's grandmother, the last active keeper, working out of Ashwatch)
and Orrin reach the diagnosis that the closure will later encode: the chain
was fighting the drift, and pressure was building behind it like water behind
a dam.

**The Winter of Two Storms — 31 years ago.**
*First storm.* The drift turns violent. The Reed floods and moves at the mill.
Hessa, Silas (then lead carter) and the Compact **close the road**: closure
marks cut at every route stone — *two weaves and a break* — and the chain
snuffed from the shrine down, "not two dark in a row" reversed into "dark on
purpose, at intervals, like a fence."
*Between the storms.* The autumn amber train is late and heavy. The crew —
five carts, five drivers, a tally-man — refuse the closure. The official
excuse is weather; the real argument is money and pride: they read the closure
as Hall panic to hold the amber price up. They go in. Silas goes as far as
the Frozen Pond and turns back. It is the sentence he has been finishing ever
since.
*Second storm.* The Tangle folds around them. Not dramatically. The trees
move the way trees move out there, only faster, and the road they were on is
not a road anymore.

**30 years ago — the warden and the last page.** Searchers go in. Hessa and
Orrin build the Ashwatch warden in one hard month to stop them (see §8 for how
a village makes a stone thing that move). Hessa writes the ledger's **last
page** — and it is not a death list (see Twist 1). Mara is born the same year;
the shrine lamp has been cold since before she was born, and she has never
seen it lit until now.

**~30–5 years ago — the Lean Wicks.** No trains. No amber crews. Trade shrinks
to the hill road and the Tops carters, twice a season. The Commons grows grass
over the ruts. The square lamp goes out sometime in there and nobody admits
who let it. Silas turns to building houses instead of driving. Finn inherits
the mill and the wandering river. Mara inherits the ledger and the oil list.
Elara arrives to study why the plants in the drift are the way they are and
stays.

**Now.** The Tops carters bring a traveler up the hill road. The drift is the
calmest it has been in thirty years. The route stones still hold their
rubbing.

## 6. The peoples beyond

**The Compact carters.** Road families, half of them related to someone in
Hearthwick. Their hall in Tarrow still lists the lost crew as **"on the
road"** — not dead. Not sentiment. Compact bookkeeping: a tally is settled at
the Count House, and until it is, the roll stands. Their sign is a wheel over
a candle. They are the ones who brought the player up.

**The Sennick ferrymen.** Chain-ferry, sour about the whole lantern business,
generous with anyone who turns up wet. They cut their mooring chain when the
crossing turns and never cut it twice — a second cut means the family has
moved the ferry, and the Sennicks have moved the ferry three times in living
memory because the Reed kept moving first.

**The amber crews.** Seasonal washers from Tarrow and the Tops who follow the
tide. They walk by stone, not by path. They are the only people who are
*comfortable* in the drift, and they will tell you it is not courage, it is
low expectations: "you don't find your cairn, you build another one." Elara's
whole method is theirs.

**The Tops folk** (Holt End, Nine Acre). Charcoal, smallholding, hardy,
punctual. They still come over twice a season. They are Hearthwick's
newspaper, its nail supply, and its occasional midwife.

**The Tarrow Hall and Count House.** Not villains — clerks. The Hall price is
real and grinding, and the closure cost Tarrow as much as it cost Hearthwick
(barge knees and storm-grade amber stopped arriving), which is why the Count
House's books have a standing entry nobody in Hearthwick knows about yet.

## 7. How the wider world reaches the player

Everything above has to arrive without the player leaving the map.

- **Season-marked dialogue.** Lines rotate with the mark (Orrin about frost
  heave in Mudrise, Finn about the freshet, Elara about the tide weeks).
  The Turning notice is a date, so dialogue can key off it exactly.
- **The notice board.** Projects, festival notices, Turning announcements,
  and the small ads of a village that thinks nobody is listening (see §9).
- **POIs in the Wilds.** Count Post, Amberwash, Barge Landing, Compact cairn,
  Oak-Mark Stump, the Last Camp. Each one is a piece of the road economy, not
  just atmosphere.
- **Trinkets.** Toll tokens, tally-sticks, oak-mark seals, candle hulls —
  micro-lore with a currency stamp on it.
- **Journal.** The player's voice does the connecting: token in the moss →
  Count House → "this token is warm."
- **Village projects.** The north bridge is the hill-road bridge: finishing it
  is what makes the Tops carters a regular event again. The old book buried
  under the footing is the Compact's roll with the lost still marked *on the
  road*.
- **Echo POIs at the Turning.** The phantom camps in the outer Wilds are
  glimpses of something still in progress (Twist 1), not ghosts.

## 8. Fixing the warden while I'm in the timeline

The owner's decision stands: village-built, after the Two Storms, to stop
people following the lost. The open question was *how a village makes a stone
thing that moves.* Answer: **they didn't make a creature. They made a lamp.**

- Orrin's joinery: an iron-oak frame and a hollow stone body — quarried blocks
  hauled up the hill by everyone in the village, which is why the folklore
  says "the village built it" and means it.
- Hessa's part: a sealed core of **storm-grade amber** in the chest cavity,
  kept burning behind glass and iron, trimmed by nobody for thirty years and
  still alight. That is what "keeps" it.
- The physics is the same as the road: a lit lamp holds its land to its shape.
  The warden's flame is fixed on one shape — *the road is closed here* — and
  so the warden *is* that boundary. It moves the way the boundary moves: it
  seeks the edge of what it is holding. It has no opinions to test you with.
  It is not a golem. It is a very heavy sentence with a light in it.

This also explains why a rubbing of the closure mark satisfies it: the mark is
the same instruction, cut in stone. Show it the sentence it is keeping and it
steps aside to let the sentence be amended. And it explains why it stops now,
in play: the player is amending the closure, not breaking it.

## 9. My two twists

### Twist 1 — the account is still open (the lost caravan is late, not dead)

The obvious version of the Lost Expedition is a tragedy: they died out there,
we find their things, we mourn, the world is sad. The draft even gestures past
this with "echoes" of phantom camps. I want to go one step further and make it
**bookkeeping**:

**The caravan never settled its tally, so the Compact never struck it off the
roll, and every few years the Count House in Tarrow punches a receipt from a
Hearthwick train that is not on any schedule.**

Five carts, dry bread, cold tea, wood the Count House clerk notes is
"unseasoned" — timber that has been in the drift thirty years and is still
green. They come out at the wrong end of the road. They settle in full, with
interest. They turn around and go back. The drift is not a monster that ate
them; it is a longer road than anyone thought, and they are still on it.

Hessa knew. The ledger's last page is not names — it is **shares, tallies and
a standing order**: keep the account open, keep one lamp lit at Ashwatch "for
the last cart," don't strike them off the roll. Mara has kept the instruction
for thirty years thinking it is grief. It is logistics.

**Why this beats the obvious version:**

1. It makes the wider world *load-bearing*. The answer to the central mystery
   lives in a place the player never visits — in a punched token, a tally-
   stick, a clerk's note copied onto a scrap. That is the whole point of my
   brief: places that shape this place without being visited.
2. It stays cozy. Nobody was eaten by anything. They are commuting. The tone
   line is "danger is mild and defeat is a stumble" — being thirty years late
   is a stumble, and a warm one.
3. It gives the reveal order real payoff. Trinkets (toll token stamped
   *Hearthwick train*, warm in the moss) → POIs (the Last Camp, five bedrolls,
   five sets of tracks) → journal ("Hessa was not writing a memorial. She was
   keeping books") → the buried Compact roll under the north bridge → the
   Echo POIs at the Turning, which stop being ghosts and become *sightings*.
4. It gives the relit road a future beyond the credits: when the chain is lit
   again, the long way round becomes a way home. The endgame beat is not
   "the darkness recedes." It is "the last cart is coming."

### Twist 2 — the Turning is on the notice board (the reset is a date, not a technical event)

The design says outer regions reset on a schedule, announced on the notice
board a day ahead. That is a server constraint. I want it to be a **village
custom** as well: the Wilds turn at the **dark of the wick**, posted in the
keeper's hand, and the whole village reacts on a calendar.

- The day before: cairn-walkers clear their stones; Elara packs her camp;
  Silas moves the timber pile off the Commons edge "just in case."
- The day of: nothing dramatic. A wind change (the existing
  `SEASON_SHIFT_NOTICE`), and by morning the charts are wrong.
- The day after: new echoes, new gravel in the Amberwash, the notice board
  carries what moved.

Why better than the obvious: the obvious version is a modal saying "the Wilds
shifted," which is a system message wearing a costume. A dated turning gives
players a reason to read the board, gives NPCs a schedule to be charming about
("Elara has packed. It must be the dark of Amber-wick"), and turns the reset
from a loss (unclaimed loot gone) into an *occasion*. Also: it keeps the
magic-as-weather rule — the Turning is a timetable for weather that runs
through land.

## 10. Disagreements with draft 1

1. **Who built the warden, and when, contradicts itself.** Timeline step 4
   has the warden instructed at the closing; §3 says it was placed after the
   expedition; the flagged change says "30 years ago after the Two Storms."
   Fix the order: Two Storms → closure → the caravan goes in between the
   storms → warden built the following month to stop the searchers. My §5
   timeline is the corrected sequence and everything else should key off it.
2. **Silas contradicts himself.** Timeline has him helping close the road as
   lead carter; his bio has him on the expedition with survivor guilt; the
   rewrite has him "drove the last cart out of the Tangle *before* the Winter
   of Two Storms." All three can be true only in one order: he drove the last
   lawful train out before the first storm, helped cut the closure marks, then
   went *back* in with the amber train and turned back at the Frozen Pond.
   Recommended line (replaces the rewrite):
   > "I drove the last cart out before the second storm. Bram drove the one behind me. Nobody drove the one behind that."
3. **The expedition "refused to heed the closure mark" but was "lost during
   the Two Storms."** The closure happens *because* of the first storm, so the
   refusers must have gone in *between* the two storms. Spell it in that order
   every time, or the mystery reads as a plot hole.
4. **Light-vs-dark framing slips back in.** The bible rightly flags it and
   then the copy says "the dark lantern road," "a point of gold against the
   dark weave." Fix the metaphor: **lit = remembered, dark = unremembered.**
   The Wilds are not evil, they are *unsettled* — closer to a survey that
   keeps failing than to a curse. Suggested: Brackenwood tagline "Cool shade,
   leaning stones — and things that hop" is perfect; the ruin tagline
   rewrites are good; anywhere "dark" means "dangerous" should say "forgot
   its shape" instead.
5. **"The miller's dad" vs Finn.** Pip's line references an older miller; we
   now have Finn. Make it Finn's father, old Fen of Reed Mill, who is exactly
   the sort of man who would name a stone warden and then go back to work.
6. **POIs "Old Shrine" and "Abandoned Cart" are still thin.** The rewrites
   help. Mine help more if each POI carries a *function*: the shrine is the
   crews' offering stone (leave something that grows), the cart is a
   countable train with a tally-stick in it. See §11.
7. **The last page of the ledger as a death list is the weakest idea in the
   draft.** A list of names is a memorial, and memorials stop gameplay. A
   tally with a standing order is an *active thread*. See Twist 1.
8. **"Decades" and "thirty years" are doing all the work.** Give the calendar
   and the dated timeline so dialogue can be specific ("thirty-one years, one
   wick" is much better than "a very long time ago").
9. **Hearthwick is called isolated but Mara talks about "the carters."** It
   is only isolated from the *river*. The hill road and the Tops carters are
   the surviving link and should be named in every "how did this get here"
   answer.

## 11. Concrete content

Everything below is drop-in shaped for the systems in `world.ts` and
`expansion-writing.ts`. Dialogue lines are ≤ 160 characters.

### Dialogue — new lines for existing and new NPCs

**Finn (the miller)** — `NEW_NPC_LINES.finn` / conversation stages:

- "The Reed moved again last night. My wheel is racing in mud. Do you know what that does to a millstone?"
- "We say 'as the Reed goes' like it is charming. It is not charming at three feet of drift."
- "Father named the warden. Said it does its job. So do I. The river does not."
- "Tarrow took our flour by barge once. Now the landing is a meadow with a mooring ring in it."
- "If you float a candle hull at the Breaking, float it downstream of my wheel. I am begging."
- *Mudrise:* "The freshet is early. Again. Every year the ice goes out and takes a foot of bank with it."
