# Take: glm — THE STRANGE

*Focus: the rules of the drift and of anchor-light, how the lanterns and the
stone warden were made and work, the Echoes, what seasons do to the outer
Wilds, what the Tangle and its POIs really are, trinket stories, and how the
Lost Expedition is revealed through the game's systems.*

My stance in one line: the magic of Fingersnap should be **remembering** —
the drift is the world forgetting, lanterns are the world being reminded, and
every system the game already has (ledger, journal, trinkets, Echoes,
notice board, fallen-hero lanterns) is secretly about keeping memory alive.

---

## The two twists, plainly

1. **Anchors are made of memory, not magic.** An anchor-light is three parts:
   amber-oil (the Wilds' own congealed memory), a *naming* spoken over the
   flame (the keeper telling the lantern exactly what it is holding still),
   and iron and glass (just the lamp). A lantern without a naming burns but
   anchors nothing. This makes the ledger, Mara's job, the journal, the
   trinkets, and the Echoes all load-bearing lore instead of flavor — and it
   rhymes with the real premise (your real-world progress becomes embers;
   remembering becomes light) without ever saying so out loud.
2. **The stone warden moves only when unobserved — and that is not a mystery,
   it is the craft.** Everything in the dark Wilds moves when no one is
   watching, because nothing holds it. The warden is a piece of *named* land,
   so it also moves when no one is watching — but it moves on purpose, one
   slow step at a time, always back into the gap, because its one instruction
   is "the road is closed." It is the drift's obedience made visible. No
   worthiness test, no ancient guardian: just a decision still being kept.

Everything below hangs off these two ideas.

## 1. The rules of the drift

The Wilds is not haunted and not cursed. The drift is its climate; lit
lanterns are a microclimate. The village doesn't have a physics of the drift —
it has sayings. Under each folk rule I give the working truth, the kind of
thing Elara half-proves and Orrin refuses to write down.

| Folk rule (village saying) | Working truth |
|---|---|
| "The land forgets what it isn't reminded of." | Drift is the default. Memory is the anomaly. Hills and paths wander unless held. |
| "Watching buys you an hour; light buys you a road." | Attention slows the drift; firelight fixes it. A lit lantern is a promise the land keeps. |
| "Pinned ground is paid for in ground elsewhere." | Anchoring doesn't cancel drift, it displaces it. Over-light one road and the drift piles up like water against a weir. |
| "Cold keeps; the White Quiet gives back." | Deep-drift ground preserves what it takes — objects, impressions, sometimes whole camps. Winter is when it surfaces them. |
| "Never pin two hills in one week." | The keepers' craft includes spacing. Relit too fast, the road builds pressure again. |

Two drift speeds matter for play:

- **Slow drift** — the ordinary kind. Paths wander a few feet a season.
  Forage patches climb hills. Maps go stale in days. (Pip's maps are a running
  joke precisely because Pip is the only person who keeps remaking them.)
- **Fast drift, "a stir"** — during storms and season turns, unanchored land
  moves in minutes. This is what grows a root through an axle in a single
  night (Abandoned Cart) and what "walked the hills" in the Two Storms.

**Why the road stayed dark for thirty years:** not just that no one relit it —
parts of it can no longer be relit, because a naming needs someone who
remembers what the lantern held, and the keepers who knew the far road are
gone. Recovering lost namings is the long game of the whole expansion
(see Echoes, §6).

### Anchor-oil, and why embers are precious

The oil is rendered from **amber** — the woods' own memory, congealed. Sap is
a tree's remembering; amber is the Wilds remembering itself, and the keepers
used the Wilds' substance to pin the Wilds in place. Amber comes from the
**Sapping Grounds**, two seasons' ride out past the map's edge, and that road
has been dark since the Two Storms. Oil is rationed. This is the in-world
reason your embers are scarce and every spend means something: *warmth banked
is a lantern owed*, as Mara's column-headings say.

**Iron-oak** is the drift read backwards: it grows ring-dense only where old
anchors stood, because the land held still while it grew. Elara reads stumps
like calendars. A tight-ringed stump in the deep Wilds means a lantern stood
there once, and no one in Hearthwick remembers lighting it. That is a quiet
kind of treasure: a stump is a namings-page that survives even when the
ledger doesn't.

## 2. How an anchor-light is made (and what a hollow light is)

An anchor-light has three parts:

1. **The oil.** Amber, cut with tallow, rendered by the keeper. Burns slow,
   smells faintly of resin and honey.
2. **The naming.** The keeper tells the lamp what it holds: *"You are the
   third turning above the creek. Behind you the hazel stand, ahead the
   shrine. Keep the weave tight."* Not a spell — closer to a postman
   memorizing a route. The lamp keeps the road by being told the road.
3. **The lamp.** Iron frame, glass, wick. The least important part, and the
   only part Orrin will admit matters.

A lantern with oil but no naming burns **hollow**: it lights, it warms,
it anchors nothing. There are hollow lights still burning in the deep Wilds —
lit long ago by someone who named them wrong, or by someone who had nothing
left to name but hope. (See the Hollow Light POI, §5.)

The keepers' Ledger of the Road looks, from the outside, like a lamp-oil
ledger — counts of tallow, columns of pints. The middle pages are the road
itself, told lantern to lantern: every naming on the route, in the keeper's
hand. **Mara's grandmother, Hesper, was the last Keeper, and Mara is the
Keeper now whether she has admitted it or not.** When Mara says "the ledger
doesn't keep itself," she is understating her job the way a lighthouse keeper
might say "I do the windows."

The closure mark, *two weaves and a break*, is ledger-script: a direction
mark says *go*; a closure mark is a thread **cut on purpose**. The warden is
the knot that keeps it cut.

## 3. The Winter of Two Storms — a clean chronology

This fixes draft 1's contradiction (was the warden "instructed" at the
closure, or built after?) and gives the tragedy a human-sized cause.

- **Before the road.** The Wilds drifts as it always has. Foragers pay the
  wood at little way-shrines. Iron-oak is prized and rare — which is to say,
  there were anchors here before there were keepers.
- **The Weaving.** Carter guilds and keepers build the Lantern Road together:
  lamp-stones every bowshot, namings recorded in the Ledger. Trade blooms.
  On nor'east winds you can hear Twoford's market bells checking on us.
- **The Weir Years.** The guild wants more lit-night running; the keepers
  comply. The road is pinned straighter and kept lit later into the year than
  the drift likes. Pressure builds the way it does upstream of a weir.
  Rennick, lead carter, cuts a road-nail for every year the road runs clean.
  He has eleven.
- **The last good autumn.** Signs Hesper reads and the guild does not: sap
  running wrong, iron-oak dropping boughs on windless nights, kettle-ponds
  freezing in patches in October.
- **The closing.** Hesper closes the road — cuts the closure mark at the
  Commons and at Ashwatch, and posts the warden's *commission* (below). The
  guild objects; a season's trade, walked away from. Rennick argues longest,
  and kindly, and loses.
- **The push.** The caravan stages at the Commons anyway — the deep ruts are
  theirs. Silas is among them: he argued to turn back, was voted down, and
  drove the light cart as far as the Mossy Arch before the second storm
  made the argument for him. They sent him home with the empty cart and the
  heaviest share of the luck.
- **The first storm** is a white-out. The caravan shelters at the arch and
  waits it out properly: wood stacked, canvas lashed, boots square by the
  fire, a game of fox-and-geese scratched into the stone. You do not wait out
  a storm you believe will kill you.
- **The second storm** is the stir. Three nights of fast drift — the night
  the hills walked. The road forgot itself under the carts. A root grew
  through a rear axle in hours. They broke camp and walked, and the Wilds
  took the road in behind them like water closing over a stone.
- **After.** In one winter, Hesper and Orrin build the warden at Ashwatch —
  not to guard anything, but so that no one follows. The last page of the
  Ledger is headed **"Owing"**: names, and beside each name what the road
  owes them. And the village chooses a kind story — *the weather was bad* —
  because half the village is guild family, and because Hesper refused to let
  the last thing said about Rennick be "he argued." The oil caravans stop
  going out; the Sapping Grounds road goes dark; the oil is rationed; the
  road sleeps.

## 4. The stone warden

**What it is:** the shrine path's oldest waystone, wearing a stone coat.
You cannot name a brand-new stone to a place it has never been — a naming
needs a memory to hold — so Hesper and Orrin used the one stone that knew the
road best. Orrin fitted a figure around it in one winter and complains, to
this day, that it was the worst joint of his life: *a joint that doesn't
move.*

**How a village made a stone thing that moves:** it doesn't walk. It obeys the
drift-rule from the inside. Unwatched, the dark Wilds drifts — and so, one
half-step at a time, does the warden, always back into the gap, always
facing downhill. No one has ever seen it move. Everyone has seen that it has:
Hesper's ledger holds a sketch of it standing at the ruin's east arch thirty
years ago. It now stands west. It has crossed the ruin the way a fence post
leans — too slow to catch, too steady to doubt.

**The encounter is a correction, not a fight.** The warden has one instruction
and no curiosity: *the road is closed.* Touch the closure mark wrong and it
presses you — slowly, irresistibly, the way a current does — back down toward
the ruin. Nobody "defeats" it. You show it the rubbing, the cut thread, the
reason it stands there, and it has nothing left to say; it steps aside the
way a man steps aside when told his watch is wrong. (The runtime's
`defeat-guardian` event stays; the fiction above it changes. The rewrites doc
already leans this way — "it won't fight you, but it won't let you pass
easily either" — this finishes the job.)

Why this is better than the obvious version (an ancient golem testing your
worth): no test, no worthy, no mysticism. Just a decision still being kept,
which is sadder, stranger, warmer — and strictly village-made: oil, names,
and Orrin's worst joint.

Sample lines (≤160 chars each):

> **Orrin:** "You want to know how stone walks. It doesn't. You look away, it
> doesn't. Same as the woods — but stubborn on purpose."

> **Orrin:** "Hesper named it. I carved it. Worst joint I ever made. It's the
> only one that's never leaned."

> **Pip:** "I watched the warden all afternoon once. It never moved. BEST day.
> Orrin says that's the point. I say it's manners."

> **Mara:** "Grandmother's ledger looks like oil counts. The middle pages are
> the road, told lantern to lantern. The last page is the names."

> **Mara:** "I read the last page once. It isn't a list of the dead. It's
> headed 'Owing.' I couldn't read past that."

## 5. The Tangle and what its points of interest really are

**The Tangle is a scar.** It is the stretch where the pinned road snapped and
heaved in the Two Storms. After the closure it drifted the hardest, and its
drift is *tangled* — memory broke into small pieces and got misfiled — which
is why paths there double back and a hill in the morning is a hollow by dusk.

Draft 1 calls its trees "older than Hearthwick." They can't be; the scar is
thirty years old. The fix is better than the mistake: **the Tangle regrew
from roots, not seeds.** The old road's roots survived the storm, and the
root-web still remembers the ruts it grew along — old roots, young trunks.
The paths misbehave there because the ground keeps re-suggesting a road that
was closed under it. Elara: *"Everything above ground is thirty. Everything
below is older than me."*

### POI discovery texts (proposed rewrites; current text in parentheses)

**Old Shrine**
> A crumbling stone altar, moss-soft on top. A neat handful of acorns — two
> winters old at most. Somebody still pays the wood for the picking.
*(was: offering left "a very long time ago" — contradicted by the custom
below.)*

What it really is: a foragers' way-shrine, pre-road. The last place on the
old route where you stopped lighting lanterns and walked the last stretch
dark, leaving the Wilds its own ground. The acorn payment is the custom —
*pay the wood for the picking* — and the payer these days is Elara, who will
not say so and does not need to.

**Fallen Waystone**
> Half-buried, pointing firmly into the heart of an oak. Either the stone has
> lied for thirty years, or the tree is standing where the path used to be.
> Both, likely.

What it really is: a direction stone the road drifted out from under. It
still points along the old road's memory, and the memory has since drifted.
Read it with the drift, not against it: this stone is how you learn that a
map here is a rumor with good posture.

**Frozen Pond**
> Cloudy ice, thick even now. Under it, a carter's glove — a work glove,
> taken off for a fiddly job and never fetched. The pond is keeping it cold
> for them.
*(was: "lost during a sudden frost" — which the rewrites doc already
contradicts with "no carters were lost to mere frost." Resolved: the glove
isn't a death. It's a work-glove. Someone meant to come back for it.)*

What it really is: deep-drift ground preserving things the way winter does —
the last ordinary moment of the expedition, filed away.

**Abandoned Cart**
> The axle isn't worn through — it's grown through. One root, one night's
> work. The cargo is gone, carried on ahead: nobody flees and packs the good
> cloth first.

What it really is: the second storm's signature — fast drift, wood moving
like weather — and the evidence that the caravan expected to return. They
unloaded the good cargo before they walked out.

**Mossy Arch**
> A lamp-arch over the old road. Fresh cart tracks run in under it, and none
> run out. Scratched into the stone: a game of fox-and-geese, mid-match.

What it really is: the last anchored gate before the Tangle, and the
caravan's shelter during the first storm. Silas stood here. The whittled fox
stood watch on Maren's cart wheel. The tracks stop because the second storm
came before the argument was finished.

### Two new POIs

**Hollow Light**
> A lantern, burning, in the deep of the Wilds where no road runs. It lights
> nothing. Whoever trims that wick has been trimming it a long time.

What it really is: an anchor-light with a wrong or worn-out naming — the
craft's failure mode. Whether it is kept by a lost hand or by some habit of
Hesper's far lights is left alone. Some lamps should keep their reasons.

**The Quiet Camp** (an Echo site — see §6)
> A camp kept neat as a purse: wood stacked, canvas lashed, boots set square
> by the fire. Waiting-out weather, not fleeing it. The ashes have never been
> rained on.

## 6. Echoes

**What an Echo is:** the drift holds impressions the way still water holds
reflections — firelight and grief and songs sink in and stay. When an outer
region turns (the game's epoch reset), ground that held something must give
it back, and old impressions surface for a while, the way frost heaves
stones up out of a field. The village calls them Echoes. They are not ghosts
and not people; they are the land's kept memories, and they only ever show
the caravan doing ordinary things, because that is what they mostly did:
camped, mended, waited, kept each other company.

**Why this beats draft 1's "phantom camps":** draft 1 gives no mechanism and
no player verb — watching ghosts is passive and spooky-adjacent. With a
mechanism, Echoes become the game's tenderest system instead of its
spookiest, and they plug straight into systems that already exist:
season-turn resets (the design's epochs), POI charting, and — for the
settling verb — fallen-hero lanterns.

**The Echo window.** Echoes appear in the days around a region's turning
(the notice board already warns a day ahead). Winter turns surface the
sharpest Echoes — cold keeps; the White Quiet gives back — footprints,
voices, a tune nearly whole.

**Settling (the player verb).** At an Echo site the player can spend embers
to light an *owed lamp* — the same craft as the fallen-hero lanterns the
village already tends for travelers who stumble. An Echo with its lamp lit
doesn't vanish; it *finishes*. The kettle boils. The tune completes. A voice
says something small and warm, and the memory files itself away kindly. The
village phrase is *giving them their light* — Hesper's custom, once a season,
one lantern beyond the ledger's count, every solstice after the Two Storms.
Now it's the player's custom, and the village's.

Sample Echo vignettes (discovery text, ~one line each):

> You smell woodsmoke first. By the fire a girl teaches a reel on a dented
> tin whistle, slower every line, until the tune is right. The kettle never
> quite catches.

> A woman sews a torn hood by a small fire, swearing at the thimble in a
> friendly way. She checks the sky the way carters do — twice — and goes
> back to sewing.

> Someone sets a small whittled fox on a cart wheel and says something you
> cannot hear. One laugh, from inside the canvas. Then the woods remember
> something else.

> (settled) Tonight the kettle boils. The tune finishes. A voice, warm and
> ordinary: "That's us, then." The fire burns down the way fires do, and the
> wood remembers them kindly.

Settling beat, as ember-dialogue at an Echo lamp:

> **Echo Lantern:** "The Echo circles its cold fire the way a moth circles a
> window. It isn't lost. It's waiting for light that's owed to it."
> → *Light the owed lamp:* "The flame takes, and the Echo settles toward it
> like a cat toward a warmed chair. Somewhere in the drift, a kettle begins
> to sing."

## 7. What the seasons do to the outer Wilds

The design's outer regions reset on a schedule, announced on the notice
board a day ahead; each new epoch carries a season. In-world: **the Wilds
turns**, and each turning deals the region a new temper. The village calendar
names the four turns:

- **The Thaw (spring).** The Wilds sloughs winter. Paths sink and reappear
  elsewhere; everything smells of sap; things buried surface. Muddiest
  walking of the year.
- **The Green Hush (summer).** Drift slows — growth is itself a remembering;
  the woods are busiest being trees. Safest season for the deep Wilds.
  Elara camps out. Fresh amber can be pried.
- **The Shedding (autumn).** The Wilds drops what it is done with. Maps go
  wrong fastest; old paths close; windfall leaves pile in the wrong valleys.
  Harvest what you can, early.
- **The White Quiet (winter).** Cold is stillness: drift nearly stops, the
  Wilds holds its breath, and snow preserves impressions — Echoes come
  sharpest. But winter is also when the Wilds settles its accounts and gives
  back what it held. The Two Storms were a giving-back that came as a flood
  instead of a trickle. The keepers' whole craft is arranging for it to
  trickle.

Notice-board texts (the board is Mara's; the voice is the village's):

> **THE WILDS WILL TURN** on the last day of the month. Take nothing out you
> cannot carry back. Tell someone where you walked.

> *Thaw:* paths sink and reappear. Mind the soft ground. Report any road
> that comes back.

> *Green Hush:* the drift sleeps light. Good foraging. Pay the wood at the
> shrine — someone always does.

> *Shedding:* maps go stale fast. If your landmark is gone, stand still and
> count to fifty.

> *White Quiet:* the Wilds gives things back in winter. Bring anything found
> to Mara before you keep it.

(Replaces the flat `SEASON_SHIFT_NOTICE` with per-turn variants; keep the old
line as the generic fallback.)

## 8. Trinket stories

Draft 1's trinkets are "things someone left in a hurry," which is generic
loot flavor and doesn't survive the question *why are these in chests?* My
fix: **trinkets are what the Wilds gives back.** Deep-drift ground keeps
objects; at turnings, trinkets surface. Each belonged to a named carter, and
each blurb has two stages — *found*, and *after you have witnessed its Echo* —
so the same object ripens as the mystery does. The carters:

- **Rennick**, lead carter. Argued longest, kindly. Eleven road-nails.
- **Maren**, wheelwright, Rennick's wife. Kept the convoy's candles.
- **Tansy**, the convoy's youngest, learning the roads and one reel.
- **Hollis**, Rennick's crew and the miller's uncle. Carried a luck-bead.
- **Silas**, who turned back at the arch, and has never once said so.

Two-stage blurbs:

**Tin Whistle**
> *Found:* Dented on one side, as if dropped — or thrown, once, in a temper.
> It still plays a clear note, and slightly smug.
> *After:* Tansy's. She was learning "The Thawing Reel." In the Echo she gets
> it right. You catch yourself humming it on the road home.

**Spare Bootlace**
> *Found:* Leather, waxed against weather by someone who expected weather.
> *After:* Rennick's. He waxed bootlaces for the whole convoy the night
> before. He planned to be out in it. Just not out in *that*.

**Beeswax Candle**
> *Found:* Smells of honey and old dust. Never been lit. Saved for something.
> *After:* Maren's. In the Echo it is lit at last, stuck in a bannock for a
> birthday. The Wilds was holding it for her. Now it holds the memory instead.

**Whittled Fox**
> *Found:* Soft pine, lopsided ears — more a joke of a dog than a fox.
> Knife-work you'd know anywhere.
> *After:* Orrin's knife; Silas's hand; Maren's cart wheel, where it stood
> watch over the cargo. Silas hasn't asked for it back. He asked you to keep
> it out of the rain.

**River Glass Bead**
> *Found:* Frosted green glass, river-smoothed. A luck-bead, the kind you
> leave when you mean a thing.
> *After:* Hollis's. He meant a thing. The miller's father never said his
> brother's name, but he kept a windowsill for the bead to come home to.

**Harness Bell** (new)
> *Found:* A cart bell, green with age, clapper tied quiet with a bootlace
> end.
> *After:* Maren rang it twice at the arch — once for leaving, once for
> meaning to come back. At turnings the Wilds rings it sometimes, to say it
> is still keeping them.

**Road-Nails** (new)
> *Found:* Eleven iron nails in a twist of cloth, each head stamped with a
> year.
> *After:* Rennick's — a nail for every year the road ran clean. The twelfth
> was cut but never stamped. Mara has stopped calling them the lost.

## 9. Elara and Finn, in drift terms

Both new NPCs should be *symptoms of the drift with jobs*, not lore vending
machines:

- **Elara** studies shifting plants and is quietly writing the drift's real
  rules (the table in §1 is hers, uncredited). Her camp sits one turn from
  permanent — she moves it with the Green Hush. She pays the Old Shrine and
  dares anyone to make something of it. Key findings: iron-oak rings
  (§1), the Tangle's root-memory (§5), and that blue moss is the drift's
  own anecdote — *it won't poison you, it just makes you forget your own
  name for an hour, which is worse.*
- **Finn's** river wanders because nothing down-valley is named anymore —
  the valley floor's old anchor went dark with the road, and water keeps no
  promises it isn't reminded of. His mill problem *is* the drift problem,
  which makes the mill-wheel village project the first place the whole
  village pays the Wilds back a little. Sample lines:

> **Finn:** "The river used to keep to its banks. Nothing down-valley is
> named now, so nothing holds. Why would the water stay?"

> **Finn:** "Mill runs crooked three days a season. I set the stone by the
> drift calendar now. Hate that I have a drift calendar."

## 10. The Lost Expedition: the reveal spine

The mystery unfolds system by system; each beat changes what *old* content
means rather than only adding new content. (What the player learns → how.)

| # | Beat | System |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | The road was closed on purpose | Closure-mark rubbing (demo quest), Orrin |
| 2 | People were lost, not just a road | Trinkets surfacing (whistle, bootlace); Pip's warden lines |
| 3 | The closure followed an argument | Commons ruts + Orrin's guild story; journal connects them |
| 4 | They didn't die on the road — they're *kept* | First Echo at a season turning |
| 5 | The village chose a kind story | Mara's ledger middle pages vs. what they omit |
| 6 | The Wilds is holding their things | Frozen Pond glove, unlit candle, road-nails — all "meant to come back" objects |
| 7 | The keeper never stopped waiting | Solstice Echo: young Hesper at the Commons leaving one lamp lit all night |
| 8 | They can be settled, not saved | Owed lamps at Echo sites (ember spend) |
| 9 | The debt is the road | Ledger's last page; "The Keeper's Light" village project |

Key journal entries (player voice):

> *after the first Echo:* "Saw the camp by the pond — or its memory. They
> weren't running. Wood stacked neat, boots by the fire. You don't wait out a
> storm you believe will kill you."

> *after cart + pond:* "The cart was unloaded before the root took the axle.
> The glove was taken off for a fiddly job. Everything here says the same
> thing: we meant to come back."

> *after the warden yields:* "The warden doesn't guard. It remembers. One
> instruction, kept thirty years: the road is closed. I showed it the
> closure mark and it stood aside like a man told his watch is wrong."

> *after the last page:* "The last page of the ledger isn't a list of the
> lost. It's headed 'Owing.' Names, and beside each, what the road owes.
> Maren: one candle, lit. Rennick: one road, relit. Tansy: one tune,
> finished."

> *final:* "We lit the quiet camp tonight and the Echo finished itself —
> kettle, tune, laugh, done. The Wilds was never keeping prisoners. It was
> keeping guests. And guests are owed a lamp on the path home."

**The capstone project — "The Keeper's Light."** A late village project
(ember + material costs, shared): clean, fill, and relight the far
lamp-stones past Ashwatch, one hill at a time, as the ledger's rationing
allows. Completion changes the horizon: on clear nights, a second point of
gold on the far hill.

> *Open:* "The ledger lists lamp-stones past the hill, dark these thirty
> years. A road is a promise. We are behind on ours."
> *In progress:* "The far stones are cleaned and filled. Oil is rationed, so
> we light one hill at a time."
> *Complete:* "On clear nights you can see it — a second point of gold on
> the far hill. Pip has named it. Orrin pretends he cannot see it."

**Fallen-hero lanterns, unified.** The multiplayer system (a lantern left
where a player stumbles; another member relights it) gets its origin here:
any lantern lit for someone else is a *keeper's light* — Hesper's solstice
custom, carried on. The existing `FALLEN_HERO_LANTERNS` lines need no
change; this is the why beneath them.

## 11. Places beyond the map

The plan asks for places that shape the world without being visitable. Mine:

- **The Sapping Grounds** — amber country, two seasons out. Source of all
  anchor-oil; road dark since the Two Storms; hence rationed oil, hence the
  whole ember economy has an edge to it.
- **Twoford** — the far market town. Thirty years of hearsay; its bells are
  said to check on Hearthwick when the wind sits nor'east. Prices, fashions,
  and the guild's old grievances all come from there.
- **Highcart Rise** — the guild's home hill. Guild families are half of
  Hearthwick; the kind story about the weather is as much for them as
  anyone.
- **The Ravel** — the deep Wilds, where ground changes faster than a person
  can watch. Not sinister; simply unreadable. Even Elara turns back at the
  fence-posts she planted last summer, which are now a day's walk apart.

## 12. Disagreements with draft 1

   system instead of inventing a ghost mechanic.
