# The Chronicle of the Lantern Road
## The Comprehensive History of Hearthwick, the Drift, and the Last Cart

The magic of the Wilds is not a spell. It is simply the act of remembering. 

In the Wilds, the default state of the world is a slow, restless amnesia known as the **Drift**. The land forgets what it isn't reminded of. When no one is watching, hills wander, paths slip away, and rivers change their course. The Wilds are not evil, haunted, or cursed; they are merely unsettled. The drift is their weather.

---

### Part I: The Anatomy of the Drift and the Anchors

The village of Hearthwick does not have a physics of the drift; it has sayings. *"The land forgets what it isn't reminded of"* is the foremost. *"Watching buys you an hour; light buys you a road,"* is the second.

**The Two Speeds of Drift**
1. **Slow Drift:** The ordinary kind. Paths wander a few feet a season. Forage patches climb hills. Maps go stale in days. 
2. **Fast Drift (The "Stir"):** During violent storms and seasonal turns, unanchored land moves in minutes. This is what grows a root through a cart axle in a single night, and what "walked the hills" during the Winter of Two Storms.

**The Craft of the Keepers**
To live in the Wilds is to constantly remind the world of its own shape. Long before Hearthwick existed, foragers and wanderers learned that attention slows the drift, but firelight fixes it. The people of this land fought the drift using **Anchor-lights**. 

An anchor-light is not a magical artifact, but a mechanism of memory constructed in three parts:
1. **The Oil:** Amber-oil, cut with tallow, rendered by the Keeper. It burns slow and smells faintly of resin and honey. Amber is the woods' own memory, congealed.
2. **The Naming:** The most crucial element. The Keeper must speak the exact route to the flame (*"You are the third turning above the creek. Behind you the hazel stand, ahead the shrine. Keep the weave tight."*). It is not a spell; it is telling the lamp exactly what it is holding still.
3. **The Lamp:** Iron frame, glass, and wick. The physical vessel.

A lantern with oil but no naming burns **"hollow"**—it gives warmth and light, but it anchors nothing. But a properly named lamp pins the land in place. The Keepers' *Ledger of the Road* looks like a mundane accounting of tallow, but its middle pages contain the road itself, told lantern to lantern: every naming on the route.

**The Flora of Memory**
**Iron-oak** is the drift read backward. It is so dense that green timber sinks in water. It grows tight-ringed only in places where old anchors stood, holding the land perfectly still while the tree matured. A tight-ringed stump in the deep Wilds means a lantern stood there once, acting as a quiet kind of treasure—a naming's page that survives even when the ledger doesn't.

### Part II: The Company Town and the Wider World

One hundred and fifty-five years ago, Hearthwick was founded as a company town. It sat perfectly positioned between the deep amber reserves of the **Sapping Grounds** and the slow-growing **iron-oak** forests. The village's purpose was to harvest this wood and amber and send it east to the town of **Sallow Ford** (where oil was pressed) and west over the steep Tops to **Tarrow-on-the-Reed**, the great river market.

**The Commodities**
*   **Iron-Oak:** Tarrow barge-builders pay silly money for natural crooks ("knees"). Cutting is chartered by the **Oak Hall** in Tarrow. An **oak-mark** (a lead-and-wax seal in the Hall's pattern: a wheel and a wave) must be stamped into the stump before the second cut. Cutting unmarked standing oak is known as **"stealing shade"**—robbing future generations of a tree that takes two lifetimes to mature. Windfall is free to take, but custom dictates leaving *three leafy branches at the stump* so the next woodcutter knows the fall was natural.
*   **Amber:** Sold by the "drop" (a small stone weight) and graded by what it can hold alight:
    *   *Candle-grade:* Keeps a flame's light a few minutes after the flame is out.
    *   *Hearth-grade:* Lasts an hour. Used in village window-lamps.
    *   *Storm-grade:* Lasts overnight. Extremely rare and highly prized.
*   **Glims:** A glim is a bead of amber cut to the Count House's measure, holding a glint of light. The Compact paid its lamplighters in them, and Hearthwick never stopped. Glims don't fade. Raw amber is still sold by the drop; a glim is amber someone has already cut and lit.

**The Carters' Compact**
To ensure the trade flowed safely, the **Carters’ Compact** and the Oak Hall funded the creation of the **Lantern Road**. Keepers placed lamp-stones every bowshot. The toll was a **wheel-tax**: a copper per wheel per lantern-lit league, receipted with a punched token. 

Every carter swore to the Compact's three duties at Carting Day:
1. **Light:** Never leave the chain dark. The last cart out lights behind itself and checks the wick ahead.
2. **Log:** Log your load at the Count House. No unlogged amber, no unmarked oak.
3. **Leave:** Leave the road as you'd wish to find it. Cairns topped, ruts drained, gates shut.

The golden rule of the road was absolute: *never two dark in a row.* For decades, this brought peak trade—the Great Carting.

### Part III: Customs, Calendar, and the Turning

**The Calendar (The Twelve Wicks)**
Up here, a month is a **wick**—the stretch between one trimming of the road's lamps and the next. The twelve wicks run: *Thaw, Mud, Bud, Bloom, Light, Cart, Haze, Sap, Amber, Leaf, Smoke, Quiet.*

The seasons are called **Marks**, named for road work rather than weather:
*   **Mudrise (Spring - The Thaw):** Breakup and freshet. Paths sink and reappear. The village celebrates **The Breaking**, floating *candle hulls* (walnut-shell boats with leaf sails) down the river Wend when the ice goes out.
*   **Carting (Summer - The Green Hush):** Long light. Safest season. Drift slows because growth is itself a remembering. The village holds **Carting Day** (Cart-wick the 6th). Once a great fair, it is now a bittersweet market day where an empty polished horse collar (hame) is hung on the Commons gate for the cart that never came home.
*   **Amberfall (Autumn - The Shedding):** The sap tide. The Wilds drop what they are done with. Maps go wrong fastest. The village celebrates **Amberwake** on the first night of Amber-wick. Every house sets a hearth-grade window lamp, and the Long Table is set with the *Empty Chair* for those lost in the Tangle.
*   **Quiet (Winter - The White Quiet):** Cold is stillness; the drift nearly stops. The anniversary of the road's closing falls here on **Closure Night**, where every lantern in the village is lit, but *the road beyond the last post is deliberately left dark.*

**The Turning**
The outer Wilds do not drift on a whim; they turn on a schedule the village has learned to read. The Keeper posts the date a day ahead on the notice board (*"Dark of Amber-wick — the outer Wilds will turn."*) Cairn-walkers clear their stones, charts reset, and the landscape shuffles. It is a ferry timetable for a river that runs through land instead of water.

**Sayings of Hearthwick**
*   **"Two weaves and a break."** The closure mark. Used in daily speech to mean a final decision made and kept. (*"You said you'd stop drinking cider. Two weaves and a break, now."*)
*   **"That's the Hall price."** Non-negotiable. Also used to describe stubborn people.
*   **"Green wood sinks, dry wood sings."** A warning for patience. Hurried work drowns.
*   **"Leave one for Ada."** Leave the door unlocked or a light on for someone who is late.

### Part IV: The Winter of Two Storms

Pinning the ground is paid for in ground elsewhere. Anchoring the Lantern Road did not destroy the drift; it displaced it. By keeping the road rigid year-round, pressure built up against the edges of the light like water against a dam. This was the "Weir Effect." The outer Wilds began to turn violently. The woods were suffocating under the rigid memory of the road.

Thirty-one years ago, the pressure broke. 

**The First Storm and the Closure**
It began with a ferocious white-out. Keeper Wenna Hale, realizing the road was burning through its oil reserves fighting the violent drift (*"Oil, 3 casks short,"* her ledger read), made the hardest judgment call of her life. She closed the road. She cut the closure mark—*two weaves and a break*—into the route stones, snuffing the chain from the hilltop shrine downward. *"Dark on purpose, at intervals, like a fence."*

**The Lull Run**
Then came a nine-day lull of slack calm. Hollis Brack, the lead carter, argued that nine days was enough to run a train to Sallow Ford for the oil they desperately needed. Wenna refused. But on the dawn of the sixth day, a crew went *under the mark* anyway. Hearthwick calls them **The Six**:
1. **Hollis Brack** (41): Lead carter. He laughed with his whole back and carved wooden foxes for every door he delivered to.
2. **Tam Cooley** (34): Ox-driver. Talked to the oxen more than to people.
3. **Bett Cooley** (34): Tam's twin. Sang flat on purpose to annoy Tam.
4. **Dorrit Venn** (20): Orrin’s bridge-building apprentice. On her first day, she set the village signpost crooked.
5. **Joss Penhallow** (16): A runner. He dented his tin whistle to a specific note you could hear across the square.
6. **Nan Greer** (50s): Wenna's lamplighter. She read route marks the way other people read faces.

There was supposed to be a seventh: **Silas**, Hollis's husband. But at the Brackenwood fork, Silas heard the wind change, felt a sudden terror, and turned back. He told the village his knee gave out. He has limped on his left knee ever since so no one would ask the truth.

**The Second Storm**
The second storm hit three days early. It was a "stir"—fast drift, where wood moves like weather. The Tangle is the scar of that night. At the crossing, the ground beneath Orrin’s great bridge shifted and tore apart. **Bett Cooley** and **Dorrit Venn** fell with the carts and were lost. 

The remaining four—Hollis, Tam, Joss, and Nan—pushed forward. They made it to Sallow Ford. But behind them, the Wilds rushed in like water over a sinking stone. The road forgot its shape. The chain went completely dark.

### Part V: The Warden, The Echoes, and The Secret

**The Stone Warden**
The spring after the storm, searchers wanted to go in. To stop them, Wenna and Orrin built the **Stone Warden** at Ashwatch in eleven hard days. 

It is not an ancient golem; it is a lamp wearing a stone coat. Orrin built a frame out of iron-oak—salvaged from Dorrit’s ruined second span—and hauled drift-stone to cover it. Wenna sealed a core of precious storm-grade amber inside its chest. They stood it on the path, arms outstretched, and kept watch for a full night so the stone "learned" its shape. 

Because drift-stone moves when unobserved, the Warden walks. But because of its amber heart, it only ever walks back to its programmed pose. It is desperately homesick for one position. Wenna gave its heart a single naming—*the road is closed here*—and cut the same words beside the closure mark on the Ashwatch route stone. Copy them down, turn them round, and speak them to the lamp in its chest—*the road is held again; rest*—and you tell the flame it may stop holding the break. The latch opens, the flame gutters, and the Warden settles to rest. It stopped Silas from following Hollis that second spring, and Silas and Orrin have never spoken of it since. Orrin resets the village signpost to Dorrit's exact crooked lean every spring.

**The Deep Drift and The Echoes**
The deep drift preserves objects and impressions in a state of "White Quiet." When the outer Wilds turn, the land gives these memories back. 
*   **Trinkets:** The Wilds spit out objects meant to come home: a dropped work glove, an unlit beeswax candle saved for a birthday, Tam's knotted ox-halter, and eleven stamped road-nails.
*   **Echoes:** Phantom camps appear—not ghosts, but the woods replaying the mundane, waiting moments of the Six. A player can settle these Echoes by spending glims to light an "owed lamp." Lighting the lamp allows the kettle to finally boil, the tune to finish gracefully, and the memory to file itself away kindly. 

**Wenna's Secret**
By the third autumn, a carved pine fox washed down the river Wend and caught in the mill grate. It had a long right ear. Silas always carved his foxes mirror-wise, with the long ear on the left. Hollis carved them with the long ear on the right. 

The miller, Aldo Tolley, brought it to Wenna. She knew instantly: the four had survived. But she also knew that if she told the village, they would march into the dark Wilds and die, for the road could not be kept. Silas had told her, *"If you've news, I don't want it unless it's walking."* 

So, Wenna kept the secret. Every autumn, a new fox washed down the Wend. Aldo kept them in a linseed box. Wenna added a tally mark to the last page of her ledger, written in Keeper's script. She died eight years ago, leaving the unreadable page.

### Part VI: The People of Hearthwick Today

Today, Hearthwick is a village of people deciding what to do with grief that is thirty years late. 

*   **Mara Hale (27):** Inherited the ledger and the rationing of oil. She runs the "ledger soup" in the square for anyone working on a village project. She is terrified that if relighting the road is possible, her grandmother Wenna left it dark out of cruelty. She possesses the unreadable last page and says *"Noted"* when she disagrees—a habit she unconsciously picked up from Wenna's margins.
*   **Pip Penhallow (12):** The village runner. Pip explores the woods mapping the "Pencil Map" (because the lines drift) looking for the perfect note to dent into their whistle, unknowingly following the old notches left by their uncle Joss.
*   **Orrin (60s):** The gruff bridge-builder who measures in fingers and keeps a boxwood folding rule with *D.V.* scratched on the back. He complains about the "Hall price of hinges" to hide his grief over his lost apprentice, Dorrit.
*   **Silas (64):** Retired carter and builder. He carves door-foxes for every new lintel in town, all mirror-wise. He limps on a perfectly good knee because he cannot admit he turned back out of fear.
*   **Elara Quill (30s):** A forager who pretends to be studying the drift, but actually drifted in from the **Merrow Saltings** two summers ago on a spring flood-drift. In the Saltings, they don't fight the drift with lamps; they ride it using *drift-tables*. She is secretly stranded, waiting for her sister Bryony's light to guide her home.
*   **Finn Tolley (36):** The anxious miller. He counts the wandering turns of his waterwheel (*"Forty-one and holding"*). Two winters ago, his father Aldo died, leaving Finn the linseed box containing twenty-seven wooden foxes and a note meant for a dead Keeper. He carries a secret he doesn't fully understand.
*   **Ada Cooley (88):** Mother of the twins. She has kept a lamp burning in her window every single night for three decades. The village pays for her oil. She never grieved; she simply waited correctly.

### Part VII: A Light That Isn't Ours

The arrival of a traveler via the steep western hill road (The Low Road) changes the moral pivot of the world. Because the traveler has the power to relight the lanterns, the road becomes holdable again. 

The mystery of the Lost Expedition stops being *what happened to them*, and becomes *bookkeeping*. Because the Six never settled their tally at the Tarrow Count House, the Compact never struck them off the roll. They are still considered "on the road." Wenna's unreadable last page is not a memorial list of the dead; it is a ledger of shares, tallies, and a standing order: *keep the account open.*

As the player pushes deeper, mending Orrin’s bridge with warden-stone (creating a bridge that walks back to its place after shifting), settling the Echoes, and relighting the chain, the profound truth is revealed. 

When the easternmost lanterns finally flare to life on the distant hills, a second point of gold can be seen blinking back from the horizon. 

It is the Sallow Ford lamp. Hollis Brack has kept it lit for thirty years. The account is still open, and the last cart is finally coming home.
